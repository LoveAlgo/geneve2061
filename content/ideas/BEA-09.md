vision: >-
  In 2061, Geneva’s building law asks for beauty, not just the absence of mistakes. Every new building
  is designed to deserve heritage listing on the day it is finished, and its quality is checked with residents
  and with the measurements described in [[BEA-08]]. Architects engrave their names on their buildings.
  Ugly buildings are repaired, re-clad or, at the end of their life, replaced with buildings people love.

today_headline: >-
  Geneva’s building law contains an aesthetic clause, but it can only refuse an “architectural error”
today_detail: >-
  Article 15 of Geneva’s law on construction (LCI) allows the canton to refuse a building that would harm
  the character or interest of a neighbourhood, based mainly on the opinion of the Commission d’architecture,
  whose role is consultative. Courts treat it as a negative aesthetic norm: only an architectural error
  can justify a refusal. Yet the law already speaks of the harmony of the city’s silhouette and of the
  perception of its topography.

view: |-
  In most countries, beautiful old buildings are protected by heritage listing, for a reason: people appreciate beautiful buildings. So why do cities still allow buildings to be built that would never meet heritage standards?

  Architecture is public art: it affects everyone who walks past, and most people have not asked for eyesores that increase their stress. If architects had to engrave their names on their buildings, as they did on many Paris façades in the 19th century, they would not build anything they were not proud of.

  If my reasoning is right, the vast majority of the world’s buildings need to be fixed or replaced. The good news is that we need not worry about unemployment for the next hundred years, until all the world’s buildings have been brought up to the standards of Vitruvius and Christopher Alexander.
