vision: >-
  In 2061, Geneva is easy to find your way around, for residents and visitors alike. The lake, the Jet
  d’Eau, the Salève and the Old Town on its hill can be seen from many places, and each neighbourhood
  has its own landmark and character. Clear, consistent signs and maps showing walking times stand at
  every station and square, and metro, train and bus lines carry names that tell you where they go. University
  campuses and large public buildings are laid out logically, with clear names, colours and symbols, so
  that students and staff can keep their minds on their work. And children and newcomers learn early how
  the city is organised: the right bank and the left bank, Plainpalais between the centre and Carouge,
  the Old Town on its hill.

today_headline: Geneva is replacing the letters of its regional bus lines with numbers
today_detail: >-
  To comply with federal rules, Geneva’s public transport operator (tpg) is gradually giving its lettered
  bus lines numbers, with a range of numbers for each sector. By the end of 2026, line A to Gy is due
  to become line 36, line E to Hermance line 30, and line G to Corsier line 35. In 2019, the tpg acknowledged
  that their mix of letters and numbers no longer followed any logic.

today_facts:
- >-
  Campuses are often hard to read: at the University of Sheffield, a 2023 consultation found that only
  4% of students and staff recognised the colour zones on the campus signs. One person pointed out that
  in a brand-new building, the floors were labelled with letters, the rooms with numbers, and the timetable
  used a third system (University of Sheffield, 2024).
- >-
  Confusing buildings have a cost: an often-cited study estimated that wayfinding problems at Emory University
  Hospital in Atlanta cost it about US$220,000 a year, as people who are lost interrupt staff to ask for
  directions (Zimring, 1990, cited in Behavioral Sciences, 2014).

view: |-
  Universities are places where people are supposed to think clearly, yet most campuses are so confusing that even students and staff who go there every day get lost. Building N65? Z34? I am convinced that a university that fixes its orientation will see clearer thinking, and eventually a better ranking. Once a campus is built, it is hard to fix, but that is just as true of whole cities: the next generations of planners and architects will have to repair the poor work of the last few.

  Geneva is lucky: the mountains, the lake and the Jet d’Eau make it easy to find your way. Flat cities like Los Angeles need other solutions, such as logical street layouts and naming, and neighbourhoods with their own architecture. Old World cities that grew organically can be confusing, but they have far more charm than the numbered streets and avenues of Midtown Manhattan. As for buses, why does bus E go to Hermance and bus A to Gy, while bus G goes to Corsier? Bus H should go to Hermance, and bus G to Gy. And one thing nobody really thinks about: teaching people how to find their way in their own city.
